# Where the health numbers come from

_Last updated October 13, 2026._

wr1t3r's planner can work out daily targets for calories, water, protein, fat, carbs, fiber and steps, and add up the calories and nutrients in what you eat. This page lists every source behind those numbers. Each one is a government nutrition reference, a professional society's position statement, or a peer-reviewed study or review.

**These numbers are estimates, not medical advice.** The formulas describe the average healthy adult. Your own needs can be different, especially if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, have a health condition (kidney, heart or liver disease, diabetes, an eating disorder), take medication that affects appetite or fluids, or are under a doctor's care. If a doctor or dietitian has given you different numbers, use theirs.

## What it asks you

Sex, age, height, weight, how active you are, whether you want to lose, keep or gain weight, and whether you're pregnant or breastfeeding. All of it stays in your own notes. wr1t3r only works out targets for adults (18 and over), because the formulas weren't made for children and teenagers.

If you skip this step, the planner uses the standard values from the Nutrition Facts label instead: 2,000 calories, 50 g protein, 78 g fat, 275 g carbs and 28 g fiber, plus 88 oz of water. Those are reference numbers for reading food labels. They aren't meant as anyone's personal target.

## Calories

- **Calories your body burns at rest:** the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. Mifflin MD, St Jeor ST, et al. _A new predictive equation for resting energy expenditure in healthy individuals._ American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 1990;51:241–247.
- **Why that equation:** a systematic review found it the most accurate of the common equations for both normal-weight and obese adults. Frankenfield D, Roth-Yousey L, Compher C. _Comparison of predictive equations for resting metabolic rate in healthy nonobese and obese adults: a systematic review._ Journal of the American Dietetic Association, 2005;105:775–789.
- **Activity:** the resting figure is multiplied by 1.2 (mostly sitting), 1.375 (about 30 minutes of activity a day), 1.55 (about an hour) or 1.725 (two hours, or hard training). These are the standard multipliers for this equation, and the descriptions follow the activity levels in the National Academies' energy guidelines (below).
- **Losing or gaining:** 250 or 500 calories a day less, or 250 more. The national obesity guidelines recommend a 500 to 1,000 calorie daily cut for steady loss. Weight loss slows as you go (Hall KD, et al. _Quantification of the effect of energy imbalance on bodyweight._ The Lancet, 2011;378:826–837), so when your weight moves 5 lb, wr1t3r offers to work your targets out again.
- **Floor:** never under 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,500 for men (1,350 for anyone who'd rather not say). National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. _Clinical Guidelines on the Identification, Evaluation, and Treatment of Overweight and Obesity in Adults,_ 1998.
- **Pregnant or breastfeeding:** no calorie cut. Your needs go up, so ask your doctor or midwife how much.

## Water

About 101 oz a day from drinks for men and 74 oz for women (88 oz for anyone who'd rather not say). These are the National Academies' adequate intakes from drinks: 3.0 and 2.2 liters, about 80% of the total water a body needs. The rest comes from food. National Academies of Sciences. _Dietary Reference Intakes for Water, Potassium, Sodium, Chloride, and Sulfate,_ 2005.

You need more in hot weather and when you exercise. If a doctor has told you to limit fluids, follow that number.

## Protein, fat, carbs and fiber

These show when you turn on _Protein, fat and carbs targets too_.

- **Protein:** grams per kilogram of body weight. Above a BMI of 25, wr1t3r counts only the weight that would be a BMI of 25 for your height, so extra body fat doesn't raise the target. It uses the highest of these that applies, but never more than 35% of your calories:
  - 0.8 g/kg, the recommended daily allowance for adults (National Academies, energy and macronutrients guidelines, below).
  - 1.0 g/kg from age 65. Bauer J, et al. _Evidence-based recommendations for optimal dietary protein intake in older people: a position paper from the PROT-AGE Study Group._ JAMDA, 2013;14:542–559.
  - 1.1 g/kg when pregnant or breastfeeding (National Academies).
  - 1.2 g/kg while losing weight, to keep muscle. Leidy HJ, et al. _The role of protein in weight loss and maintenance._ American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2015;101(6):1320S–1329S.
  - 1.4 g/kg for two hours a day or hard training. Jäger R, et al. _International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: protein and exercise._ Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition, 2017;14:20.
- **Fat:** 30% of calories, inside the recommended range of 20–35%.
- **Carbs:** the calories left over. wr1t3r warns you if that comes to less than 130 g, the daily minimum the guidelines give.
- **Fiber:** 14 g for every 1,000 calories.

The ranges, the protein allowance, the carb minimum and the fiber figure all come from the National Academies of Sciences. _Dietary Reference Intakes for Energy, Carbohydrate, Fiber, Fat, Fatty Acids, Cholesterol, Protein, and Amino Acids,_ 2005.

## Steps and sleep

- **Steps:** 8,000 a day under age 60, 7,000 from 60. A meta-analysis of 15 studies found the drop in risk of death levels off around 8,000–10,000 steps for younger adults and 6,000–8,000 for older ones. Paluch AE, et al. _Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts._ The Lancet Public Health, 2022;7:e219–e228.
- **Sleep:** 8 hours. Adults need at least 7. Watson NF, et al. _Recommended amount of sleep for a healthy adult: a joint consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society._ Sleep, 2015;38(6):843–844.

## Calories in food

- **Built in:** USDA's _SR Legacy_ food table, about 7,800 common foods (no brands), shipped with the app and searched on your device. U.S. Department of Agriculture, Agricultural Research Service, FoodData Central, 2018. It's public domain.
- **Search USDA:** where it's set up, the Food button also searches all of USDA FoodData Central, which includes branded foods with their label serving sizes.
- **Your own Nutrition Database:** every food you log comes from a table in a note called _Nutrition Database_, in your own Dropbox or Google Drive. Picking a USDA result adds it there. You can also type in your own foods as new rows, or use _Sync to Nutrition Database_ on a recipe note to add the recipe with its nutrition worked out per serving. Each row is a food, a serving, then calories, fat, carbs, protein and fiber.

Calorie counts on food labels and in databases are averages. Real food varies, and US labels are allowed to be off by up to 20%.
